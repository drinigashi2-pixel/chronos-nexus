# Chronos Nexus — Autonomous Interstellar Civilization Optimization

Chronos Nexus is a C++17 system that runs an interstellar civilization on a large, changing galaxy
graph. Every simulated day it decides how resources move between planets, which spacecraft flies
which mission on which route and when, what infrastructure to build with a limited budget, and how
to react to unexpected events. It works within a fixed time and memory budget and tries to keep its
own computational cost low.

The problem breaks into several NP-hard subproblems. No single algorithm solves the whole thing, so
the system combines exact algorithms, approximations and metaheuristics behind a meta-optimization
layer. All core algorithms are implemented from scratch with the STL only.

> **Report status.** All measured numbers in §9 and §13–§17 come from `results/summary.csv` and
> `results/raw/` of one complete experiment run on the hardware listed in §13 (3 seeds per size,
> official budgets). The tables in §14–§15 and the observed column of §9 are generated by
> `scripts/fill_readme.py`; no number in this file was estimated.

## 3. Formal problem definition

**Galaxy.** A directed graph `G = (V, E)`. Each vertex `v ∈ V` is a location (planet, colony or
station) with coordinates. Each edge `e = (u, v)` is a route with distance `d_e`, travel time
`T_e ∈ [t_e^min, t_e^max]` with mean `t̄_e`, fuel `f_e`, risk `ρ_e ∈ [0,1)`, capacity `κ_e`,
reliability `r_e = 1 − P(failure on e)` and availability `a_e(t) ∈ {0,1}`. Attributes change over time.

**Planets.** For each `p ∈ V` and resource `k ∈ R = {Food, Water, Energy, Fuel, Medicine, RareMinerals, Construction}`:
production `π_pk`, consumption `γ_pk` (per day), stock `s_pk(t)`, capacity `S_pk`, plus population
`N_p(t)`, technology, defense and strategic importance `σ_p`. Each resource has an importance `w_k`.

**Fleet.** Each ship `j` has a location, cargo capacity `Q_j`, fuel tank `F_j` with current fuel,
speed `v_j`, availability time `α_j`, reliability `ϱ_j`, operating cost `c_j` per hour and a
capability mask.

**Missions.** Each mission `m` is `(o_m, d_m, k_m, q_m, rel_m, dl_m, prio_m, imp_m, caps_m)`. Missions
come from the input, from the proactive allocator (shipments), from emergencies and from events.

**Infrastructure options.** Each option `i = (p_i, type_i, level_i, cost_i, build_i, Δπ_i, Δpop_i,
Δenergy_i, strat_i)`. Options with the same `(p_i, type_i)` are mutually exclusive. The budget `B`
is limited.

**Events.** Each event `ε = (time, type, target, severity, a, b, c, d)` has one of 12 types (see
`docs/FORMAT.md`).

**Decisions per cycle (one day).** (i) The shipments `x_{ij}^k ≥ 0` of each resource from surplus
planets to deficit planets. (ii) For every ship, an ordered sequence of missions; this fixes the
ship assignment, the departure time, the routes of the empty and loaded legs, the refuelling stops,
and which missions are delayed or rejected. (iii) The set of facilities to start. (iv) The
re-planning decisions taken when an event happens.

**Objective.** Maximize the civilization score of §12 (survival, resource security, mission success,
growth, science and expansion, minus failures, transport cost, risk, waste, energy and
computational cost), subject to the hard constraints below and the run's time and memory budget.

**Hard constraints.** The cargo of a mission fits the ship (`q_m ≤ Q_j`). The ship has the required
capabilities. Fuel never goes negative along the route; refuelling is only possible at refuel nodes.
A route is only used while it is available, and a shipment fits its capacity. A mission departs no
earlier than the ship's availability and its own release time. Each mission is assigned at most once.
Storage capacities are respected (overflow counts as waste). Construction spending stays within `B`.

## 4. Assumptions

1. **Time.** One planning cycle is one day (24 h). Times are in hours and planet rates are per day.
2. **One cargo at a time.** A ship carries one mission's cargo at a time. Missions larger than the
   biggest capable ship are split into chunks.
3. **Travel time.** Edge travel times are independent random variables, triangular on
   `[t_min, t̄, t_max]`, with `σ_e = (t_max − t_min)/4`. Path time is approximated as
   `N(Σ t̄_e, Σ σ_e²)` (central limit approximation) for the chance constraint.
4. **Failure.** A mission fails, and its cargo is lost, with probability `1 − ϱ_j · Π r_e`. One
   Bernoulli draw is made per executed mission.
5. **Route capacity.** Capacity limits the units per planning cycle on a route. A single shipment
   cannot exceed it.
6. **Refuelling.** Stations and planets holding fuel stock can refuel ships. Refuelling takes 2 h,
   costs a fixed fee and draws from the planet's fuel stock (stations are unlimited).
7. **Supply.** A planet supplies a resource only above a reserve of `reserveDays × consumption`
   (default 3 days), and only if the supply is not frozen by a `ResourceUnavailable` event.
8. **Population.** A planet whose Food, Water or Medicine has been in shortage for more than 3 days
   loses 2 % of its population per day.
9. **Knowledge.** The system does not know future events. It reacts when they happen and uses only
   the current state for predictions.
10. **Determinism.** Every random choice uses a seeded xoshiro256** generator. Every budget
    decision (strategy, iteration counts, time boxes, landmark rebuilds) is derived from the
    *nominal* budget through a deterministic work model. Each stage stops after a fixed number of
    counted search states, not after a wall-clock time. The measured clock only acts as a guard:
    2–3× the nominal boxes, never more than the cycle's fair share of the remaining time, and
    always leaving the last 5 % of the budget for writing outputs. Two runs with the same seed therefore produce
    identical plans and statistics; this was checked on small and medium. The exceptions are the
    measured time and memory values, the score's computational-cost term that depends on them, and
    runs where a guard fires.
11. **Hidden dataset.** The code assumes nothing about value ranges, connectivity or id ranges.
    External ids are mapped through hash tables, unreachable pairs are handled, and an empty mission
    list is valid.

## 5. Mathematical formulation

### 5.1 Multi-objective route cost

For a cost profile `P = (w₁, w₂, w₃, w₄, k, λ)`:

```
c_P(e) = w₁ d_e + w₂ f_e + w₃ (t̄_e + k σ_e) + w₄ (ρ_e + λ · (−ln r_e))      (∞ if a_e = 0)
```

`−ln r_e` makes reliability additive: `−ln Π r_e = Σ −ln r_e`. The profiles are:

| profile | w₁ dist | w₂ fuel | w₃ time | w₄ risk | k (σ) | λ | used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| balanced | 0.40 | 0.30 | 0.30 | 40 | 1.0 | 1 | priority 3 |
| emergency | 0.05 | 0.05 | 1.00 | 60 | 2.0 | 2 | priority 5, emergency missions |
| cargo | 0.30 | 0.80 | 0.10 | 15 | 0.5 | 1 | priority ≤ 2, empty legs, flow pricing |
| safe | 0.20 | 0.20 | 0.20 | 200 | 1.5 | 3 | priority 4, medicine |
| time (internal) | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | ALT lower bounds on travel time (deadline pruning) |

The weights put each term on a comparable scale for the generated value ranges (distance 10–100,
fuel 10–80, time 5–60 h, risk 0–0.3). For example, a risk of 0.1 under the balanced profile costs
4 units, about the time component of a 13 h edge.

### 5.2 Resource allocation (per resource `k`, solved in criticality order Medicine, Water, Food, Energy, Fuel, Construction, Minerals)

Over a horizon of `H` days (default 7), with incoming `in_p` and already planned outgoing `out_p`:

```
stock'_p   = s_pk + in_p − out_p
reserve_p  = γ_pk · reserveDays
proj_p     = stock'_p + (π_pk − γ_pk) · H
need_p     = reserve_p + extra_p − proj_p                      (extra = construction demand)
demand_p   = min(freeStorage_p, need_p + max(0, γ_pk − π_pk)·(coverDays − H))   if need_p > 0   (batching)
supply_p   = max(0, min(stock'_p − reserve_p − extra_p, proj_p − reserve_p − extra_p))   otherwise
overflow_p = min(supply_p, max(0, proj_p − S_pk))
```

The reduced transportation network has a source `S`, a fleet node `F`, supply nodes `i`, deficit
nodes `j` and a sink `T`:

```
min  Σ_{(i,j)} c_ij x_ij + Σ_j pen_j y_j + Σ_i ε z_i
s.t. Σ_j x_ij ≤ supply_i                      (arcs F→i: overflow part cost 0, rest cost ε = 0.01)
     Σ_i x_ij + y_j = demand_j                (arc j→T with capacity demand_j; y_j on shortage arc S→j)
     Σ_{ij} x_ij ≤ fleetCapacity               (arc S→F)
     0 ≤ x_ij ≤ u_ij = min(supply_i, demand_j, route bottleneck residual)
c_ij  = cost_cargo(path i→j) / refShipCap                      (trip cost amortised per unit)
pen_j = 40 · w_k · σ_j · (1 + log10(1 + N_j)/3)                (shortage penalty per unit)
```

This is a min-cost flow of value `Σ_j demand_j`, which is always feasible because of the shortage
arcs. Only the `K` (default 12–15) cheapest surplus sources of each deficit get an arc.

### 5.3 Mission scheduling

For ship `j` with sequence `(m₁, …, m_n)`, the sequence is simulated: empty leg `loc → o_m`, load,
loaded leg `o_m → d_m`, unload. The objective is

```
min  Σ_j Σ_{m ∈ seq_j} [ travel_m + late_m + chance_m + fail_m ] + Σ_{m unassigned} reject_m
travel_m = c_j (T¹ + T²)/v_j + 0.5·cost_cargo(empty) + cost_profile(m)(loaded) + refuels·fee
late_m   = 2 · prio_m·imp_m · max(0, arrival_m − dl_m)
chance_m = 4 · prio_m·imp_m · max(0, z·σ_m − (dl_m − arrival_m))    for prio_m ≥ 4, z = 1.2816
fail_m   = (1 − ϱ_j · R¹ · R²) · value_m,     value_m = 100 · prio_m · imp_m
reject_m = 4 · value_m
```

`σ_m² = Σ σ_e²/v_j²` accumulates along the ship's whole sequence. The chance constraint
`P(arrival ≤ dl) ≥ 0.9` is equivalent to `dl − E[arrival] ≥ z·σ` under the normal approximation.

### 5.4 Infrastructure (multiple-choice knapsack)

```
max  Σ_i val_i y_i     s.t.  Σ_i cost_i y_i ≤ B,   Σ_{i ∈ group g} y_i ≤ 1  ∀g,   y_i ∈ {0,1}
val_i = σ_p · [ Σ_k w_k (min(Δπ_ik, deficit_pk) + 0.25·surplusPart) · H_eff
              + w_E Δenergy_i H_eff (1 if energy deficit else 0.25)
              + 5 ln(1 + Δpop_i) H_eff/H + 10 strat_i H_eff/H ]
        + ν · [ 12 h_EG ΔP_i / (0.25 P₀) + 10 h_SP ΔT_i / (0.10 T₀) ]   (only if H_eff > 0)
H_eff = max(0, H − build_i)
ΔP_i = Σ_k w_k max(0, Δπ_ik) + w_E max(0, Δenergy_i)            (production-value gain)
ΔT_i = 0.01 strat_i τ_p + [research] 0.5 level_i (σ_p + 0.01 strat_i)   (tech·strategic gain)
ν    = Σ_p Σ_k w_k γ_pk · days / 20      (value units per score point, via the 0.20 security weight)
h_EG, h_SP = remaining headroom of the capped growth and science terms (committed builds included)
```

The second line credits the economic-growth and scientific-progress score terms directly (§12).
Both are measured on end-of-run rates, so an option counts in full once it completes before the
end. Without it the planner valued research stations at zero (they have no resource impact) and
left a large part of the budget unspent.

### 5.5 Prediction and lead time

```
t_short(p,k) = now + 24 · max(0, (s_pk − reserve_pk)/(γ_pk − π_pk))     if γ > π
t_over(p,k)  = now + 24 · (S_pk − s_pk)/(π_pk − γ_pk)                     if π > γ
lead_p       = path time + z·σ_path + 12 h margin     (from the chosen supply route)
deadline     = t_short − lead
```

For example, with 10,000 food, 700/day consumed and 300/day produced, `t_short = 10000/400 = 25`
days (minus the reserve).

## 6. Architecture and per-cycle pipeline

```
                         ┌──────────────── Meta-optimization layer (meta.cpp) ────────────────┐
                         │ features: |V|,|E|, active missions, min slack, budget left, memory,│
                         │ event severity, route variance → strategy + time boxes             │
                         └───────────────┬──────────────┬───────────────┬────────────────────┘
 events ─► Replanner ◄── DependencyIndex │              │               │
   │       (dependency-guided ALNS)      ▼              ▼               ▼
   │                         Router (Dijkstra/Bidir/ALT, fuel labels, Pareto, cache)
   ▼                               ▲            ▲             ▲
 cycle: predictor ─► min-cost flow ─► missions ─► scheduler (regret → ALNS → LS → repair) ─► infra (MCKP)
                                                        │
                                   validate ─► execute (stochastic) ─► advance planets ─► stats/score
```

`Simulator::run()` (`src/sim.cpp`):

```
setup: landmarks for cargo/time profiles; routing race ALT vs bidirectional; chunk big input missions
for day = 0 .. D−1 (stop early only if the 5 % output reserve is reached):
    deliver arrivals; finish construction; expire missions 48 h past their deadline
    PLAN CYCLE:
        predictor.rebuild → shortages within 7 days
        meta.choose(features)                                  # allocation part
        allocator.allocate (reduced MCF, trip budget)          # → shipments → chunked missions
        meta.choose(features with the new mission pool)        # scheduling part
        Pareto routes for released priority-5 missions
        scheduler.sync; candidates; strategy (B&B | regret+ALNS+LS | greedy+ALNS | local repair)
        constraint repair; validate; extract plans; rebuild dependency index
        day 0: infrastructure (60 % of the budget); day 2: growth round (down to a 10 % reserve)
    for each event in [t0, t1) in time order:
        execute plans up to the event time; handle in-flight losses
        Replanner.handle(event)  (incremental or full)       ; infra re-run for triggered planets (25 %)
    execute to end of day; advance planets (production, consumption, shortage, waste, population)
write 10 outputs + stats.json
```

The hybrid pipeline is greedy/regret-2 construction → ALNS → local search → constraint
repair/validation → incremental re-planning. Each stage can be switched off with `--no-regret`,
`--no-alns`, `--no-ls` and `--no-repair`, and `--sched` forces a single method.

## 8. Algorithm design, pseudocode and correctness

### 8.1 Routing (`routing.cpp`)

**Dijkstra (baseline)** — binary heap, lazy deletion, early exit when the target is settled.
It is correct for non-negative costs; zero-cost edges are allowed.

**Bidirectional Dijkstra (baseline).** Forward search on out-edges and backward search on in-edges,
always expanding the side with the smaller key. `μ` is the best meeting value, updated on every
relaxation whose head is already labelled by the other side. The search stops when
`top_f + top_b ≥ μ`. This is correct because any shorter path would have to contain a vertex with
both labels below the tops.

**A\* with ALT landmarks.**

```
build(profile):                     # 2L + 1 full Dijkstras
    u ← farthest node from vertex 0
    for l in 1..L:
        lm[l] ← u;  fwd[·][l] ← dist(u, ·);  rev[·][l] ← dist(·, u)
        u ← argmax_v min_l dist(lm_l, v)            # unreached components first
h(v, t) = max(0, max_l max(fwd[t][l] − fwd[v][l], rev[v][l] − rev[t][l]) − floatGuard − slack)
```

*Admissibility under dynamic costs (our addition).* Let `c₀` be the costs at build time and `c`
the current costs. For every edge changed since the build we store
`deficit_e = max(0, c₀(e) − c(e))`, and `slack = Σ deficit_e`. Any path loses at most `slack`
relative to build time, so `d_c(x,y) ≥ d_{c₀}(x,y) − slack`. Hence

```
h₀(v,t) ≤ d_{c₀}(v,t) ≤ d_c(v,t) + slack   ⇒   h₀(v,t) − slack ≤ d_c(v,t)
```

so the bound stays admissible. Cost **increases** never break admissibility and need no work.
An edge that was unusable at build time (new edge, or one restored after being closed at build
time) has `c₀ = ∞`. Such an edge is **repaired in place**: it adopts its current cost as `c₀`, and a
decrease-only Dijkstra per landmark and direction lowers `fwd[·][l]` (from the edge head) and
`rev[·][l]` (from the edge tail) wherever the new edge shortens a landmark distance. Rows for new
nodes (colonies) are appended as `+∞` first. The tables then remain *feasible potentials* of the
build costs, `fwd[y] ≤ fwd[x] + c₀(x,y)` and `rev[x] ≤ c₀(x,y) + rev[y]` on every edge, which is all
the triangle-inequality bound needs, so no table goes stale. The repair touches only the nodes whose
landmark distance actually drops (usually the new node and a few neighbours). The *stale* fallback
(bound 0, exact search) remains for the legacy interface. Rebuilds happen lazily at the next cycle
boundary, after K = 8 decreases or staleness, and only if the estimated build work (deterministic model) is below 5 % of a
cycle budget. A\* reopens nodes when a better `g` appears, so it stays optimal even if `h − slack`
is not consistent. Float storage error is guarded by subtracting `2.4·10⁻⁷ (a + b)` from each
difference.

**Fuel-constrained legs.** When the min-cost path needs more fuel than the ship has and the start
node cannot refuel, the scheduler first tries *refuel along the best route*: it walks the memoised
`route()` path and fills the tank at the current node whenever the next edge needs more fuel than
is left. That is O(|path|) after one cached query and feasible by construction; it fails only if a
needed stop is not a refuel node or an edge needs more than a full tank. On the generated galaxies
refuel nodes are almost everywhere (99.8 % of planets on extreme), so it settles nearly every leg
(extreme, one cycle: 781 label searches → 26, of which 668 had hit the label limit; on-time missions
+2.2k and failed −2.6k over the full run; the score is unchanged because the scheduler then uses
~100 s more of its time). The exact search below remains the fallback and the correctness reference.

**Fuel-constrained label search** (fallback). It is goal-directed: the heap is ordered by
`cost + h(v)`, where `h` is the ALT bound when the profile's table is exact (no slack) and has at
least 16 landmarks, otherwise the exact fuel-free distance to `t` from one reverse Dijkstra
(capacity filter applied, edges needing more than a full tank removed). With the 4–8 landmarks of
large and extreme galaxies the ALT bound is too weak and the search wandered to its label limit. Both are consistent, and refuel steps add a non-negative cost at the same
node, so the first label popped at `t` is still the cheapest one. Nodes the reverse search does not
reach are never labelled, and an unreachable target is detected before any label is created. The
reverse sweep is bounded: once it settles `s` it continues only to radius `R = 2·d(s,t) + 4·fee`,
and unsettled nodes get `h = R` (their true distance is at least the radius), so `h = min(d, R)` is
still consistent while the sweep covers a ball around `t` rather than a 100k-node graph. The
`(node, bucket)` table is a flat array holding, per node, the minimum settled cost over buckets
`≥ b`, so the dominance test is one lookup. Each search may create at most 50 000 labels; a leg
that needs more is reported infeasible for that ship, and the mission goes to another candidate.
Labels count against the scheduler's work cap, and on the extreme galaxy uncapped searches
averaged ~190 000 labels, so a handful of fuel legs consumed a whole planning cycle (measured on
the 2-core development VM: on-time missions 23.4k → 62.9k, score 54.4 → 63.1; small, medium and
large unchanged).

```
labels (cost, fuel, node, pred); heap by cost + h(node)
pop L: if ∃ settled (node, bucket ≥ bucket(L.fuel)) with cost ≤ L.cost: prune (dominance)
       settled[(node, bucket)] = L.cost                    # duplicate-state elimination
       if node = t: done
       if refuel(node) and fuel < cap: push (cost + fee, cap, node)
       for e = (node, v): if f_e ≤ fuel and not dominated(v, …): push (cost + c(e), fuel − f_e, v)
```

It is exact up to the fuel-bucket resolution `B` (16–32 buckets): labels in the same bucket are
treated as duplicates. This is a deliberate state compression.

**Bounded ε-dominance Pareto labels** (emergency and priority-5 missions). The two objectives are
time `Σ(t̄ + 2σ)` and risk `Σ(ρ − ln r)`. Labels are popped in time order. A label is dropped if it
is ε-dominated by a permanent label at its node or by a target label after adding a time lower
bound to the remaining trip (a valid completion bound: the ALT bound when the table is usable,
otherwise the exact reverse-Dijkstra distance on `t̄ + 2σ`), or if its node already holds `Lmax` (6)
labels. The safest-enough fastest path is chosen: minimum `t̄ + z σ` among paths whose failure
probability is within 1.5× of the best. Guarantee: every returned path is non-dominated within the
returned set, and the front is a (1+ε)-cover of the true front *unless* the per-node cap cut it.

**Route cache.** The key is `(src, dst, profile, capacity class)`, where the capacity class is the
smallest power of two ≥ the cargo, so a cached path is valid for any cargo up to that class. An
inverted index `edge → keys` invalidates exactly the cached paths that use an edge whose cost
increased. After a **decrease** (or a new edge `e = (u,v)`) a cached `s → t` path of cost `C` is kept
iff `LB(s,u) + c(e) + LB(v,t) ≥ C` with ALT lower bounds: then no path through `e` can beat it, and
paths avoiding `e` did not change, so it is still optimal. Only the other entries are dropped (all
of them for profiles without a usable table). The memo is a flat open-addressing index
(`FlatIndex`) into a slot vector, which replaced `std::unordered_map` on a lookup that runs tens of
millions of times per run.

**Routing race (meta layer).** At setup, 32 seeded queries are run with ALT A\* and with
bidirectional Dijkstra. The work model is settled nodes, with ALT weighted by `1 + L/8` for its
table lookups. The cheaper algorithm computes paths, and ALT tables are still used for lower
bounds. This is deterministic.

### 8.2 Resource allocation — min-cost flow (`flow.cpp`)

```
MinCostFlow.solve(s, t):                       # primal-dual successive shortest paths
    if potentials dirty: restorePotentials()   # Bellman–Ford + negative-cycle cancelling
    while flow < max:
        Dijkstra on reduced costs c(u,v) + π(u) − π(v) ≥ 0, early exit at t
        π(v) += min(dist(v), dist(t))          # keeps all reduced costs ≥ 0
        blocking flow (Dinic levels + current-arc DFS) on arcs with reduced cost 0
```

*Correctness.* Potentials stay feasible: for a settled `u`, `d'(v) ≤ d(u) + rc`; for an unsettled
`u`, `d'(u) = d(t) ≥ d'(v)`. Every augmentation is therefore along a shortest residual path, so the
flow stays min-cost for its value (no negative residual cycle). Costs are integers (×100), so the
zero-reduced-cost test is exact.

*Warm start / event repair.* Reducing an arc's capacity cancels flow through it by flow
decomposition: one path `s→…→u` is found backwards and one path `v→…→t` forwards, along
flow-carrying arcs. The potentials are then repaired with Bellman–Ford. Any cycle in the
predecessor graph is negative and is cancelled. After that, augmentation resumes. The tests check
that repair equals a fresh solve on 200 random instances.

```
allocate():
    compute supply/demand/overflow of every (planet, resource)
    for each deficit planet j, most important first (≤ half of the allocation time):
        ONE reverse bounded Dijkstra (cargo profile) collecting the K cheapest suppliers of every
        resource j lacks, with their shortest paths read off the search tree (no extra searches)
    for k in criticality order:
        arcs: capacity = min(supply, demand, fleet left, route bottleneck residual)
        solve; tighten shared-edge over-use; collect shipments (diff against materialised flow)
    enforce trip budget: keep the earliest-deadline / highest-penalty shipments (≤ free ships)
```

**Deviation from the brief, on purpose:** source discovery uses a reverse bounded Dijkstra from
each deficit, not "bounded ALT A\*" to preselected candidates. The reverse search returns the exact
K cheapest sources and their paths in one pass, and it handles wormholes. A Euclidean preselection
followed by ALT A\* would miss far but cheap wormhole sources and costs K searches. ALT is still
used for routing, deadline bounds and Pareto pruning.

The **greedy baseline** serves deficits by decreasing penalty from their cheapest sources.

### 8.3 Mission scheduling (`scheduler.cpp`)

**Candidate generation** (search-space reduction):

```
for mission m: ships ← K+8 nearest by spatial grid (capability + cargo filter)
    LB_arrival(j) = max(α_j, now) + (ALT_time(loc_j, o_m) + ALT_time(o_m, d_m)) / v_j + load
    keep j if LB_arrival ≤ dl_m + 72 h  and  fuelCap_j ≥ loadedFuel/3   (else keep the best 2)
    drop idle ships at the same node dominated in (α, Q, fuel, tank, ϱ, c, v, caps)
    keep the first K
```

**Regret-k insertion** (lazy max-heap, deadline-ordered batches of 400 so it is anytime):

```
for m in batch: best insertion delta per candidate ship (beam positions)
regret(m) = Σ_{i=2..k} (Δ_i − Δ_1)   (missing ships count as the reject penalty)
loop: pop max regret (stale versions skipped); insert at best position if Δ_1 < reject_m
      only missions listing that ship are re-evaluated (ship → missions index)
```

**ALNS with simulated-annealing acceptance:**

```
weights ← 1; T0 ← 0.05·cost/ln2
for it in 1..N (deterministic N from a work model; wall-clock guard):
    d ← roulette(destroy weights); r ← roulette(repair weights); q ∈ [2, 40] ≈ 15 % of pool
    beginUndo(); removed ← destroy_d(q, pool); repair_r(removed ∪ some rejected)
    accept if better or U < exp(−Δ/T),  T = T0·0.002^(it/N)
    score: 33 new best, 9 better, 13 accepted worse; segment of 40 → w ← 0.75w + 0.25·score/uses
    reject → rollback() in O(touched ships)
restore best (copy-on-write snapshots of touched ships)
```

Destroy operators: random, worst-cost (randomised, power 3), Shaw/related (origin + destination
distance + deadline + resource + same ship), deadline-urgent (largest lateness/chance penalty),
and dependency-guided (one ship's missions plus missions that list that ship as a candidate).
Repair operators: greedy, regret-2, regret-3. Insertion uses a beam: positions `rank ± beam` around
the deadline order, plus append.

**Local search polish:** relocate (remove and re-insert at the best place), swap between ships, and
2-opt\* (exchange sequence tails between two ships). First improvement, at most 3 passes,
time-boxed.

**Constraint repair and validator.** Missions that are infeasible under the current routes, fuel or
capabilities are removed and greedily re-inserted. The validator re-simulates every ship and checks
assignment uniqueness, cargo ≤ capacity, capabilities, departure ≥ availability and release, route
availability, route capacity ≥ cargo, and the fuel tank along every edge with the planned refuel
stops. Late arrivals are reported but are soft.

**Baselines.**

* *Greedy:* insert by (priority desc, deadline asc) at the best position.
* *Plain GA:* permutation chromosome; the decoder appends each mission to its best candidate
  ship or rejects it; tournament selection (3), OX crossover, swap/inversion mutation, elitism 2.
* *SA-only:* random relocate/swap moves with geometric cooling.
* *Exact B&B (≤ ~12 missions):* missions in deadline order. Each mission is inserted at every
  position of every ship or rejected, which enumerates each solution exactly once. All ships are
  used for fleets of ≤ 40; for larger fleets, the union of the missions' candidate ships, so the
  result is exact over that set. Children are
  explored in increasing delta order. The bound is `cost(decided) + Σ_{undecided} LB_m` with
  `LB_m = min(reject_m, max(0, c_loaded − 0.5·c_cargo) + (1 − ϱ_j)·value_m)`, and the incumbent
  comes from regret + local search.

*Correctness of the B&B bound.* Inserting a mission never decreases the cost of the already-placed
missions when travel costs and times satisfy the triangle inequality and reliabilities do not depend
on the path. The loaded leg can replace at most `0.5 × c_cargo(o_m, d_m)` of a successor's
discounted empty leg (triangle inequality), which gives `LB_m`. On such ("metric") instances the
B&B is exact: the tests compare it to exhaustive enumeration and found 0 mismatches. On general
instances (risk- and reliability-weighted costs) the bound can, in rare cases, over-prune. The B&B
is then a strong heuristic, and the report treats it that way.

*Uncertainty.* Path time is modelled as `N(Σ t̄, Σ σ²)`. Priority ≥ 4 missions are penalised unless
`dl − E[arr] ≥ 1.2816 σ`, which corresponds to P(on time) ≥ 0.9. Expected failure
`(1 − ϱ·R)·value` steers both ship choice and route choice, because the profiles carry `−ln r`.
Execution samples triangular times and Bernoulli failures.

### 8.4 Infrastructure (`infra.cpp`)

```
items ← options of the requested planets, value per §5.4
prune: drop value ≤ 0 or cost > B; per group drop options with (cost ≥, value ≤) of another
if |items| ≤ 2000: DP over ⌈cost/unit⌉ buckets (B ≤ 10⁴), dp_g[b] = max(dp_{g−1}[b], dp_{g−1}[b−c_i]+v_i)
else: hull greedy — per group the upper convex hull from (0,0); global heap of incremental
      efficiencies Δv/Δc (lazy); upgrades that do not fit freeze the group
      then best single item; then swap/fill local search over the 300 most efficient options
small certification: B&B, DFS by efficiency, bound = fractional knapsack over remaining items
```

*Guarantees.* DP is exact when costs are integer multiples of the bucket unit. Otherwise costs are
rounded up, so the result is feasible but may be slightly suboptimal. Hull greedy plus the best
single item achieves at least OPT/2: the LP optimum ≤ greedy prefix + one fractional upgrade ≤
greedy + best single item. The tests verify DP = brute force = B&B and `2·greedy ≥ DP`.

Construction materials (0.05·cost) become demand in the flow. Event-triggered re-runs (new colony,
production drop, population surge) consider only the affected planets and 25 % of the remaining
budget. On day 2 a *growth* round releases the event reserve down to 10 % of the initial budget:
unspent money scores nothing, and day 2 still leaves time for the longest builds.

### 8.5 Prediction (`predict.cpp`)

The predictor keeps a lazy min-heap of `(t_short, key, version)`. An update re-computes the 7 keys
of one planet and pushes new versions. A query pops entries up to `now + horizon`, discards stale
versions and pushes the live ones back. Lead times come from the chosen supply routes
(`time + zσ + 12 h`). The allocator batches demand to `coverDays` (10) of need, so shipments are
fuller, and subtracts incoming deliveries so the same need is not planned twice.

### 8.6 Our algorithm: Dependency-Guided Incremental ALNS (`replan.cpp`)

**Idea.** An event invalidates only the decisions that depend on the changed object. We keep an
explicit dependency index so the invalid set is found without scanning the plan. That set is used
as the seed of the ALNS destroy step, and the optimiser only works inside a small neighbourhood.

```
DependencyIndex:  edge → [(mission, planVersion)],  ship → [...],  planet(origin) → [...]
                  (+ allocator: edge → flow arcs, planet → supply/demand arcs, resource → network)
handle(event):
    apply event to the world (graph via Router.onEdgeChanged → ALT slack / cache invalidation)
    flow repair (warm start) for changed edges/planets → cancelled & added shipments
    I ← index.invalidSet(event)                        # O(entries of the touched keys)
    R ← I ∪ created missions;   cancelled missions leave the pool
    if mode = Full: unassign all; regret-2 + ALNS over everything      # comparison baseline
    else:
        remove I;  N ← R ∪ Shaw-related(R, ≤ 8|R|, ≤ 60)
        regret-2(R) within the time box
        if |R| > 15 % of active: ALNS over all missions (3× box)          # escalation
        else: ALNS restricted to N (destroy picks only from N)
    re-extract plans only for touched ships; re-index their missions
```

*Correctness / properties.*

1. **Exact invalid set.** Index entries are appended whenever a plan is extracted. An entry is live
   iff the mission is still active, its plan is valid and the plan version matches, so stale
   entries are filtered and never wrong. Every live dependency was indexed when its plan was
   created, so the invalid set equals the brute-force definition. The tests check this for 90
   random events across 8 event types and for a global freeze.
2. **Locality.** Destroy operators only remove missions from `N`, and insertion never moves other
   missions between ships. Every mission outside `N` therefore keeps its ship and its relative
   order. The tests check this for all 12 event types.
3. **Validity.** After the burst, the scheduler's constraint repair and validator hold. The tests
   check that `validate().ok()` holds and that stored plans use only available routes after every
   event type.

*Complexity.* `O(|entries(touched keys)|)` for detection, plus
`O(iterations · q · K · P · L)` for the burst, where `|N| ≪ M`. The full re-solve costs
`O(M · K · P · L)` for construction alone. The re-planning log reports `invalid`, `removed`,
`reinserted` and `unchanged` for every event.

### 8.7 Meta-optimization layer (`meta.cpp`)

| condition | choice |
|---|---|
| ≤ 12 active missions, ≤ 40 ships and > 200 ms per cycle | exact B&B |
| low budget (< 50 ms/cycle) or memory > 85 % | local repair only (greedy insertion), greedy allocation on big graphs |
| \|V\| > 5 000 or > 3 000 active missions | greedy (append-only, K = 6) + pruning + short ALNS |
| otherwise | regret-2 + time-boxed ALNS + local search |
| event with severity ≥ 0.8 and a mission due in < 12 h | re-plan with half the time box (local repair) |
| routing | ALT vs bidirectional race at setup; bidirectional under memory pressure |
| landmarks | 16 (≤ 20k nodes), 8 (≤ 200k), 4 otherwise; halved until ≤ 20 % of the memory budget |
| time split | ~10 % setup, 15 % landmarks + flow, 60 % scheduling, 15 % slack (events/overruns), last 5 % kept for outputs; planned per cycle = 0.75 × nominal budget / cycles (20 % allocation, 80 % scheduling); if the measured clock falls 2× behind the plan, the cheap regime is used |
| trip budget | shipments per cycle ≤ min(free ships, scheduling budget × rate − missions still waiting for a ship); rate = 1, 0.5, 0.25 or 0.1 insertions/ms for \|V\| ≤ 2k, 20k, 200k or larger |
| work model | every optimisation stage is capped by `budget_ms × WORK_UNITS_PER_MS` (1500) counted search states; ALNS stops at 85 % of the cap, leaving the rest for local search; cooling follows the work progress |

**Anytime behaviour.** A feasible incumbent always exists: rejecting a mission is feasible. Every
optimiser checks its work cap and the wall-clock guard, and the simulation stops when only the 5 %
output reserve is left, then writes the incumbent. The memory guard clears the route and fuel caches and can drop the landmark tables.

## 10. NP-hard components

| component | problem class | why exact is impractical | technique used | guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mission scheduling | multi-vehicle pickup-and-delivery with time windows, fuel, heterogeneous fleet, stochastic times (generalises VRPTW, NP-hard; even feasibility with deadlines is NP-complete) | search space ≈ (S·M)!-like ordered partitions; at 5 000 missions an exact method cannot even enumerate one level per ms | regret construction + ALNS/SA + local search + repair; B&B only ≤ 12 missions | B&B exact on metric instances; heuristics: feasibility guaranteed by validator/repair, quality measured by gap to B&B |
| Infrastructure | multiple-choice knapsack (NP-hard, weakly) | pseudo-polynomial DP explodes with large budgets × many groups | DP with bucket scaling ≤ 10⁴, hull greedy + LS for large sets | DP exact for integral costs; greedy ≥ OPT/2 |
| Multi-objective / constrained routing | resource-constrained shortest path (fuel) and multi-objective shortest path are NP-hard | label sets can grow exponentially | bucketed dominance (fuel), ε-dominance with bounded labels (Pareto), only used when needed | exact up to bucket resolution; (1+ε)-cover unless capped |
| Joint allocation + scheduling | coupling transport plans with fleet trips is an integrated network design / routing problem | the joint model is a large MIP | decomposition: MCF on a reduced network with trip budget, then scheduling | MCF exact for its reduced model |
| Re-planning | re-optimising after each event is as hard as the original problem | full re-solve per event costs O(M) work per event | dependency-guided incremental ALNS | locality + validity (tested) |

The polynomial components are solved exactly: shortest paths (Dijkstra, bidirectional, ALT A\*),
min-cost flow on the reduced network, and prediction.

## 11. Search-space reduction

| technique | where | counter |
|---|---|---|
| ALT lower bounds for A\*; deadline pruning of ship candidates | routing, `buildCandidates` | `nodes_explored`, `candidates_pruned` |
| K-nearest candidate ships (spatial grid) + ship dominance | scheduler | `candidates_pruned`, `dominated_ships` |
| duplicate-state elimination `(node, fuel bucket)` + dominance | fuel labels | `labels_pruned` |
| ε-dominance + bounded labels per node + completion bound | Pareto labels | `labels_pruned` |
| route memoisation + targeted invalidation | `RouteCache` | `cache_hits`, `cache_invalidations` |
| beam-limited insertion positions, append-only for very large instances | scheduler | `candidates_evaluated` |
| reduced flow network (K sources per deficit, paths from the search tree) | allocator | `candidates_evaluated` |
| dominance pruning + LP-hull in MCKP | infra | — |
| incremental re-planning | replanner | replanning log |

**Definitions used in `stats.json`.**

* `nodes_explored`: vertices settled by every graph search, including landmark builds and flow
  Dijkstras.
* `states_explored`: `nodes_explored`, plus labels created, B&B nodes, DP cells, insertion and ship
  evaluations, and predictor keys.
* `candidates_evaluated`: insertion candidates (mission, ship, position), flow arcs created, and
  infrastructure options and swaps evaluated.
* **Naive baseline** (`extra.naive_states`) =
  `states − routingSettled + routingSettled × ρ − insertionEvals + Σ slots + Σ_events active·slots`, where
  * `ρ` = (settled nodes of a plain Dijkstra) / (settled nodes of the configured algorithm),
    measured on the same queries for up to 300 sampled cache misses
    (`extra.naive_routing_ratio_sampled`);
  * `Σ slots` counts, for every insertion request, every position of every enabled ship, which is
    the work of a scheduler that evaluates all ship–mission pairs;
  * `Σ_events active·slots` counts one full regret re-insertion per event (the minimum work of a
    full re-solve).
* `search_space_reduction_pct = 100 · (1 − states / naive)`.

The naive count is partly measured (routing ratio) and partly counted analytically (slots). It is
never extrapolated from wall-clock time.

## 12. Score function and weight justification

Every term is normalised to `[0,1]` (`src/score.cpp`, definitions in `include/chronos/score.hpp`):

```
Score = 100·[0.30 PS + 0.20 RS + 0.18 MS + 0.12 EG + 0.10 SP + 0.10 TE]
      − 100·[0.10 MF + 0.08 TC + 0.08 RK + 0.05 WS + 0.04 EN + 0.03 CC]
```

| term | definition |
|---|---|
| PS population survival | surviving population / exposed population (initial + colonies + surges + facility growth) |
| RS resource security | 1 − Σ w_k·shortage unit-days / Σ w_k·demand unit-days |
| MS mission success | Σ w·(on-time delivered fraction) / Σ w over missions that ended or whose deadline passed; w = priority·importance |
| EG economic growth | clamp(Δ importance-weighted production / (0.25 × start)) |
| SP scientific progress | clamp(Δ Σ tech·strategic / (0.10 × start)) |
| TE territorial expansion | established colonies (alive, reachable, no critical shortage) / colonies |
| MF mission failures | Σ w over failed + expired / Σ w |
| TC transport cost | clamp(actual cost / naive cost); naive = dedicated round trip per delivered mission on the balanced shortest path |
| RK risk | mean expected failure probability of executed missions |
| WS waste | Σ w_k·wasted units / Σ w_k·produced units |
| EN energy | clamp(fuel used / naive fuel) |
| CC computational cost | 0.5·clamp(time / budget) + 0.5·clamp(peak memory / budget) |

**Why these weights.**

* **Lives come first.** Survival (0.30) and resource security (0.20) together make up half of the
  attainable positive score. Security feeds survival, but it also counts shortages that have not yet
  cost lives.
* **Missions are the operational output.** Mission success (0.18) is the largest of the remaining
  terms, and failures are penalised separately (0.10) because a lost cargo costs more than a missing
  one.
* **Long-term terms are capped.** Growth (0.12), science (0.10) and expansion (0.10) saturate at
  modest targets (+25 % production, +10 % tech) so they cannot dominate survival.
* **Costs are normalised against a naive baseline.** A planner that is no better than dedicated
  round trips gets 1 for the transport-cost term, and a better planner gets less. Transport (0.08)
  and risk (0.08) are weighted like a typical operating budget. Waste (0.05) and energy (0.04)
  matter less.
* **Computational cost is small but non-zero.** At 0.03 it rewards finishing early, which matches
  the competition formula `αQ − βT − γM − δR`.
* **Range.** The positive part sums to 1.0 and the negative part to 0.38, so the score lies in
  [−38, 100].

## 13. Experimental methodology

* **Datasets.** `gen_galaxy` produces four sizes: small 100/500/50, medium 1 000/10 000/500,
  large 10 000/100 000/5 000 and extreme 100 000/1 000 000/50 000 (planets/route rows/missions),
  with 3 seeds each.
  * Ships = max(20, missions). Each planet has 1–2 facility groups with 1–3 levels. There are 24,
    60, 156 and 408 events, covering all 12 types.
  * Connectivity comes from a geometric spanning tree plus random k-nearest edges and 2 %
    wormholes.
* **Budgets.** 20 s / 1 GB (small), 60 s / 2 GB (medium), 180 s / 4 GB (large) and 900 s / 8 GB
  (extreme), all over a 14-day horizon. `--budget-scale` changes them all.
* **Hardware.** Model name: 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-12450H; 12 logical CPUs available to WSL; 7.6Gi RAM in WSL; Ubuntu 26.04.1 LTS; kernel 6.18.58.1-microsoft-standard-WSL2; g++ (Ubuntu 15.2.0-16ubuntu1) 15.2.0; cmake version 4.2.3; Python 3.14.4 (single-threaded: 1 core used).
* **Experiments** (`scripts/run_experiments.py`):
  1. *scaling*: the default meta-selected pipeline on every size × seed;
  2. *algorithms*: forced schedulers (greedy, regret+ALNS, GA, SA), allocation (MCF vs greedy) and
     routing (ALT vs Dijkstra vs bidirectional) on small and medium;
  3. *ablation*: the hybrid pipeline with regret, ALNS, local search or repair switched off;
  4. *replan*: incremental (dependency-guided) vs full re-solve per event, using per-event times
     from `replanning_log.csv`;
  5. *pruning*: everything on vs `--no-pruning --routing dijkstra`;
  6. *micro-benchmarks*:
     * `--bench routing`: 200 random queries × 4 profiles × 3 algorithms, with exactness
       cross-check;
     * `--bench flow`: MCF vs greedy objective;
     * `--bench gap`: 15 instances of 6–10 missions × 3 ships, comparing B&B, regret+ALNS,
       greedy, GA and SA;
     * `--bench infra`: DP vs greedy vs B&B.
* **Metrics.** The 19 statistics, the score terms, and the per-cycle and per-event logs. Results
  are reported as mean ± min/max over seeds.
* **Theory vs observation.** Observed settled nodes, iterations and phases are reported separately
  from the asymptotic bounds of §9.

**Scalability.** Each size step multiplies planets, routes and missions by 10. Runtime grows by
×10.2 (small → medium), ×9.3 (medium → large) and ×12.0 (large → extreme): close to linear in the
instance size, against the O((n+m) log n) per query and O(M K P ℓ) per insertion pass of §9, because
routing queries are cached and the scheduler only re-evaluates candidate ships near each mission.
Peak memory grows more slowly (16 → 61 → 311 → 1289 MB, ×3.8, ×5.1, ×4.1), since small runs are
dominated by fixed buffers; at every size it stays below 16 % of the memory budget. The share of
the time budget used rises with size (3 %, 12 %, 37 % and 88 %): small and medium finish far
inside their budgets, so more time does not change them (measured: medium 60 s → 240 s, +0.15
points), while extreme uses nearly all of it.

The work saved over the naive baselines grows with size: the search-space reduction is 28 % on
small (6.2M states vs 8.6M naive; a 100-node graph has little to prune and most work is
insertion evaluation), 95.3 % on medium, 99.0 % on large and 99.6 % on extreme (1.34G vs 345G
naive). The meta layer switches regime exactly where the cost model says it should: regret
insertion with ALNS on small and medium, greedy insertion with pruned candidates and a short ALNS
on large and extreme (`cycles.csv`, column `reason`), and bidirectional search instead of ALT on
large, where it settles fewer nodes per query (235 vs 272) and is twice as fast (0.11 vs 0.20 ms).

Quality degrades gently with size: 70.7 (small), 70.1 (medium), 69.1 (large) and 63.8 (extreme).
Survival stays at or above 0.996 and hard-constraint violations stay at 0 at every size. The drop
on extreme is mission throughput: about 9 % of the missions expire (9,009) and 13,483 fail,
against 1–2 % expired on large, because one planning cycle cannot evaluate every waiting mission
within its share of the budget (see §16).

Comparisons worth noting from the table above:

* **Pruning** is what makes medium feasible: without it the run explores 5.3× more states, takes
  twice as long and the score falls from 70.1 to 45.8, because the planner runs out of its per-cycle
  work before most missions are placed. On small, where everything fits anyway, pruning only saves
  time (0.7 s vs 2.5 s; score 70.6 vs 71.0, within the seed spread).
* **ALNS vs GA**: GA completes slightly more missions (5,414 vs 5,206 on medium) but scores lower
  (69.5 vs 70.1) at 2.6× the runtime, because it trades on-time delivery and transport cost for
  coverage.
* **Incremental vs full re-planning**: incremental re-planning is 6–11× faster per event (medium:
  12.9 vs 83.9 ms average, 173 vs 338 ms maximum) and keeps 94 % of the plan unchanged, at the same
  score (70.05 vs 69.99 on medium; on small full re-planning is 0.4 points higher, inside the seed
  spread).
* **Ablation**: removing any single stage of the hybrid (regret, ALNS, local search, repair)
  changes the score by at most ±0.2 points on small and medium, which is within the seed-to-seed
  spread. At these sizes the stages overlap: each can recover most of what another misses, and the
  pruned candidate lists already make greedy-plus-repair close to the B&B optimum (§14.4: mean gap
  0.08 %).
* **Min-cost flow vs greedy allocation**: MCF removes all planned shortages (0 vs 25 / 153 / 1,684
  units on small / medium / large) for an objective 47–90× lower, at 7–18× the allocation time.

## 16. Limitations

* **Single-cargo ships.** Pickup-and-delivery with simultaneous loads (capacity sharing) is not
  modelled.
* **Approximate travel-time distribution.** The chance constraint uses the normal approximation of
  a sum of triangular variables, and edge times are assumed independent.
* **Coarse failure model.** One Bernoulli draw per mission. Failures do not destroy the ship, and
  partial loss is not modelled.
* **Capacity sharing.** Route capacity is shared across shipments of different resources (edge
  usage map), but the scheduler does not track it across concurrent missions.
* **Flow approximations.**
  * The reduced network only considers the K cheapest sources per deficit, found by the cargo
    profile.
  * Resources are solved sequentially, so this is not a joint multi-commodity optimum.
  * The trip budget is a cardinality heuristic.
* **Exactness of B&B.** The scheduling B&B is exact only under the metric/path-independent
  reliability assumption (§8.3).
* **Fuel legs.** Refuelling along the best route is feasible but not always the cheapest
  fuel-constrained path; the exact label search (the fallback) is exact only up to the fuel-bucket
  resolution and stops at 50 000 labels.
* **Deterministic work model.** The ALNS iteration budget comes from a fixed work model, not from
  calibration, so on very fast or slow machines the time boxes are used more or less fully.
* **Throughput on the largest instances.** Each insertion is evaluated with exact routes for the
  empty legs, so on extreme instances (100k nodes) one planning cycle cannot try every waiting
  mission within its share of the budget, and about 9 % of the missions expire. Fuel-constrained
  legs used to be the main cost (75 % of a cycle's work, §8.1); refuelling along the best route
  removed that, which cut failed missions by 2.6k per run but left the score unchanged, because the
  scheduler then spends ~100 s more of its time. One-to-many reverse trees for the empty legs were
  tried and rejected (large: 65.4 → 64.1, one reverse search out to the farthest of K candidates
  settles more than K point-to-point searches). Multi-threaded route prefetch (8 worker threads
  computing the next 256 missions' legs into the cache) was also tried and rejected: it evaluated
  13× more insertions per cycle on extreme, but the full run scored the same (63.61 vs 63.69) and
  took more time and 250 MB more memory, because after routing the main thread is limited by
  insertion evaluation, event repair and allocation. The planner therefore stays single-threaded.
* **Flow-repair churn.** Repair can shift flow between equivalent arcs. The diff policy cancels
  and creates only the difference, but some churn remains.
* **Landmark quality.** New edges and reopened routes are repaired in place, so tables no longer go
  stale, but cost decreases are only covered by a global slack, which weakens the bound until the
  next rebuild; rebuilds are skipped when they are too expensive for the budget. With the 4–8
  landmarks affordable on large and extreme graphs, bidirectional search beats ALT per query.
* **Memory measurement.** Peak memory is the process high-water mark (`getrusage`) and includes
  loading buffers.

## 17. Conclusions

The system decomposes the civilization problem into components with clear interfaces:

* exact polynomial algorithms where they exist (shortest paths, min-cost flow, prediction);
* exact algorithms with certification for small NP-hard instances (B&B, DP);
* approximation and metaheuristics with explicit guarantees or measured gaps for large instances
  (hull greedy ≥ OPT/2, ALNS);
* an original incremental re-planning algorithm with provable locality.

The meta layer chooses among them using the instance features and the remaining budget.
Measured on the hardware of §13, over 3 seeds per size:

* **Quality.** Scores of 70.7 / 70.1 / 69.1 / 63.8 from small to extreme, survival ≥ 0.996 and 0
  hard-constraint violations everywhere. On small and medium this is within a few points of what
  the data and the score formula allow (§12: growth and science are bounded by the facilities and
  the budget, and transport cost by the safety-weighted route profiles).
* **Search-space reduction.** 95–99.6 % fewer states than the naive baselines on medium to
  extreme; without pruning, medium falls from 70.1 to 45.8 points.
* **Exactness where claimed.** Routing has 0 cost mismatches against Dijkstra on all sizes, MCF
  and DP/B&B match their brute-force references in the tests, and the scheduling heuristics stay
  within 0.08 % on average (max 1.22 %) of the proven B&B optimum on small instances.
* **Dynamic re-planning.** Incremental re-planning is 6–11× faster per event than a full re-solve
  and keeps 94 % of the plan, at the same quality.
* **Scalability.** Runtime grows close to linearly with instance size, memory stays below 16 % of
  the budget, and every size finishes inside its time limit, also at a fraction of it (§18, time
  budget robustness).

The main open limitation is mission throughput at 100k planets, where about 9 % of the missions
expire; §16 lists the causes and the approaches we tried.

## 18. Build, run and test reference

### Build

```sh
scripts/build.sh               # or:
cmake -S . -B build -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release && cmake --build build -j
```

### Generate data

```sh
build/gen_galaxy <small|medium|large|extreme> <out_dir> [seed]
```

### Run

```sh
build/chronos --dataset <dir> --out <dir> --time-ms N --mem-mb N --seed N
  [--days N]                         override the simulated horizon (default: config.csv or 14)
  [--replan incremental|full]        our incremental ALNS (default) or full re-solve per event
  [--sched auto|greedy|alns|local|fast|ga|sa|bnb]   force a scheduler (default: meta layer)
  [--alloc auto|mcf|greedy]          force the allocation method
  [--routing alt|dijkstra|bidir]     alt = meta race ALT/bidirectional (default); others force a baseline
  [--no-regret] [--no-alns] [--no-ls] [--no-repair] [--no-pruning] [--quiet]
  [--bench routing|flow|gap|infra] [--bench-n N]    micro-benchmarks → <out>/bench_<name>.json
```

Outputs (in `--out`): `civilization_plan.md`, `spacecraft_assignments.csv`,
`resource_allocation.csv`, `mission_schedule.csv`, `route_selections.csv`,
`infrastructure_decisions.csv`, `predicted_shortages.csv`, `replanning_log.csv`, `score.json` and
`stats.json` (the 19 statistics: `total_score, population_survival_rate, resources_delivered,
resource_shortages, resource_waste, missions_completed, missions_failed, total_distance, total_fuel,
total_transport_cost, total_risk, nodes_explored, candidates_evaluated, states_explored,
search_space_reduction_pct, events_handled, avg_replan_ms, total_exec_ms, peak_memory_mb`), plus
`cycles.csv` and `convergence.csv`. The formats are in `docs/FORMAT.md`.

**Diagnostics.** With the environment variable `CHRONOS_DIAG` set, every planning cycle prints one
`DIAG` line to stderr with the split of that cycle's scheduling work: states, settled routing
nodes, fuel-search labels, insertion evaluations, candidate evaluations, route-cache misses,
refuel-along-route legs, fuel searches (found / hit the label limit) and Pareto searches. This is how
the fuel-search bottleneck on extreme instances was found. It does not change any result:

```sh
CHRONOS_DIAG=1 build/chronos --dataset data/extreme_s1 --out /tmp/diag --time-ms 193000 --days 3 2>&1 | grep DIAG
```

### Tests

`build/run_tests [name-filter]` runs the dependency-free unit tests:

* ALT A\* = bidirectional = Dijkstra on random graphs with zero weights, for all 5 profiles. This is
  also checked after cost increases, decreases, restores and new edges, together with lower-bound
  admissibility.
* ALT tables after in-place repair (reopened edges, a new colony node with shortcut edges) stay
  exact, and cached routes equal fresh Dijkstra after cost decreases and new edges (targeted cache
  invalidation).
* Fuel-label feasibility, refuel-along-route feasibility (the returned stops make every edge
  drivable, only refuel nodes are used, cost = best route + fees), and Pareto front non-dominance.
* `FlatIndex` = `std::unordered_map` under 200 000 random put/overwrite/erase/find operations with
  heavy collisions, growth and clear.
* Min-cost flow = brute force (300 tiny transport instances), = a reference Bellman–Ford SSP (150
  random networks), and warm-start repair = fresh solve. Conservation and supply checks for the
  allocator.
* MCKP: DP = brute force, B&B = DP, `2·greedy ≥ DP`, pruning keeps the optimum, and the planner
  respects groups and budget.
* Scheduler: B&B = exhaustive enumeration, ALNS gap vs exact B&B (≤ 12 missions, asserted ≤ 15 %),
  every method's output passes the validator, hard constraints hold, and pruning reduces candidates.
* Re-planning: dependency-index invalid set = brute force. After each of the 12 event types the
  plan is valid, stored routes are available, and decisions outside the repaired neighbourhood are
  unchanged. Full mode is valid, and escalation triggers.

**Time-budget robustness.** The grading machine may be slower than ours, so every size is also
run at a fraction of its budget (measured on a slower 2-core development VM, not the §13 machine) (small 0.5–2 s, medium 3–10 s, large 20–60 s, extreme 300 s). All
finish inside the limit, and quality degrades gradually (large: 68.6 at 180 s, 66.6 at 60 s, 63.7
at 20 s). Three mechanisms keep extreme inside a tight budget without dropping simulated days:
the scheduler's deadline is charged for what the cycle already spent and for the expected event
repairs of the day whenever the previous day overran its share; candidate lists in the greedy
regimes are built lazily (only for missions actually tried); and a mission whose Pareto search
failed is retried every third day instead of every cycle. Before, extreme at 300 s ran out of time
on day 9 and still finished 4 % over the limit; now it simulates all 14 days and 408 events in
285 s.

**Input robustness.** `scripts/make_edge_cases.py <dataset> <out>` derives 18 malformed or extreme
variants of a dataset (Windows line endings with a BOM, shuffled and quoted columns, each optional
file missing, header-only files, a single planet, garbage values such as `abc`/`nan`/`1e308` in 10 %
of cells, dangling references, zero-capacity/zero-speed ships, impossible missions, a disconnected
graph, a 400-event storm with unknown types and targets, duplicated rows, ragged rows). Every case
must finish with exit code 0 and a finite score; we run them under AddressSanitizer and
UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer:

```
python3 scripts/make_edge_cases.py data/small/s1 /tmp/cases
for c in /tmp/cases/*; do build/chronos --dataset "$c" --out "/tmp/out_$(basename "$c")" --time-ms 8000 --quiet; done
```

The loader clamps numeric magnitudes to ±10¹² (and subnormals to 0); without that, a single
`1e308` overflowed the first sum to ∞ (NaN score).
